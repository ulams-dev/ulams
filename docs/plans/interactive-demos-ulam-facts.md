# Ulam course: starting fact sheet (research step R0)

Appendix to `docs/plans/interactive-demos.md`, section 7.3. Compiled on 2026-10-09 from the pages listed
under "Sources", using web search and fetches. **This is a starting point, not cleared content.** The
research step M9a re-checks every row against the source it names (and against the primary source
where the row says so), then stores each fact as a citation fragment. No lesson, card or quiz item may
state a fact that is not in the cleared version of this sheet.

Markers:

- ⚠ The sources disagree. Use the wording given in the row, or leave the claim out.
- **(2nd)** The fact was seen only in a secondary source or a search summary. It needs a primary
  check before use.
- Do not use at all: wine or beer prizes in the Scottish Book, a "mimeographed" 1957 edition, and the
  name of Ulam's uncle in the Monte Carlo story. No source found supports any of them.

## 2. The Lwów School of Mathematics

| # | Fact | Src |
|---|---|---|

## 4. Monte Carlo method

| # | Fact | Src |
|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Ulam: the first thoughts came from "a question which occurred to me in 1946 as I was convalescing from an illness and playing solitaires" (quoted in Eckhardt 1987). | [17] |
| 4.2 | The question: the chance that a Canfield solitaire laid out with 52 cards comes out. Later in 1946 he described the idea to John von Neumann. | [17] |
| 4.3 | In 1947 von Neumann outlined a neutron-diffusion calculation for the ENIAC, the first formulation of a Monte Carlo computation for an electronic computer. | [17] |
| 4.4 | Nicholas Metropolis suggested the name "Monte Carlo". He gave a reason connected with a relative of Ulam's who liked to go to Monte Carlo; quote it verbatim from [18] if used. | [18] |
| 4.5 | N. Metropolis and S. Ulam, "The Monte Carlo Method", *Journal of the American Statistical Association* 44 (1949). Check the pages. | [1] |

## 5. The Teller–Ulam design (brief, factual)

| # | Fact | Src |
|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | In early 1951 Ulam proposed compressing the thermonuclear fuel, and Teller saw that radiation from a fission primary was the way to do it. | [19][1] |
| 5.2 | Their joint report, LAMS-1225, is dated 9 March 1951. | [19] |
| 5.3 | The first full-scale test of the design, "Ivy Mike", took place on 1 November 1952 at Enewetak Atoll **(2nd: confirm against a DOE/LANL page)**. | [20] |
| 5.4 | Credit for the design was disputed between Teller and Ulam for decades. Attribute any characterisation to Kenneth Ford [19]. | [19] |

| # | Fact | Src |
|---|---|---|

## 8. The Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem

| # | Fact | Src |
|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | Los Alamos report LA-1940, "Studies of Nonlinear Problems", dated May 1955. | [24] |
| 8.2 | The report's first page says it was written by Fermi, Pasta and Ulam, and the work was done by Fermi, Pasta, Ulam and Tsingou. | [24] |
| 8.3 | Mary Tsingou programmed the computation on the MANIAC I. | [24] |
| 8.4 | The energy did not spread evenly among the modes, as expected, but returned almost entirely to the starting mode (near-recurrence). | [24] |
| 8.5 | Thierry Dauxois (2008) proposed the name "Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem". | [24] |
| 8.6 | Zabusky and Kruskal (1965) explained the recurrence through solitons. | [24] |
