# Ratios & extraction

*A sour cup and a bitter cup are both maths problems. Here is the maths.*

When hot water meets ground coffee it dissolves part of it: acids first, then sugars and browning compounds, and last the heavier, bitter ones. A roasted bean is about 70 % insoluble plant fibre; at most about 30 % of its mass can be dissolved. The art of brewing is to dissolve the **right share** of that 30 %, and to dilute it to the **right strength**.

> — Maja, Gdańsk, a student from the first edition

## Two numbers that describe every cup

**Strength** is how concentrated the coffee is. We measure it as **total dissolved solids (TDS)**, the percentage of the beverage that is dissolved coffee. A good filter coffee has a TDS of 1.15–1.45 %; espresso sits at 8–12 %.

**Extraction yield (EY)** is how much of the dry coffee you dissolved:

$$
EY\% = \frac{TDS \times \text{beverage mass}}{\text{dose}}
$$

**Worked example.** You brew 18 g of coffee with 300 g of water. The grounds hold back about twice their weight in water, so about 264 g of coffee ends up in the cup. A refractometer reads a TDS of 1.35 %:

$$
EY = \frac{1.35\% \times 264\ \text{g}}{18\ \text{g}} = \frac{3.56\ \text{g}}{18\ \text{g}} \approx 19.8\%
$$

That is right in the middle of the target.

## The brewing control chart

| Extraction yield | What you taste | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| below 18 % | **Under-extracted**: sour, salty, thin, short finish | grind finer, hotter water, longer contact |
| 18–22 % | **Balanced**: sweet, clear acidity, long finish | keep it and adjust strength to taste |
| above 22 % | **Over-extracted**: bitter, dry, astringent, hollow | grind coarser, cooler water, shorter contact |

Strength is mostly about the **ratio**, and extraction mostly about **grind, time and temperature**. Change one variable at a time.

## Ratios

The brew ratio is coffee to water by weight. Weigh, do not measure with spoons: a "scoop" of a light roast weighs up to 20 % more than the same scoop of a dark roast.

$$
\text{water} = \text{dose} \times R \qquad \text{e.g. } 18\ \text{g} \times 16 = 288\ \text{g}
$$

| Method | Typical ratio | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Espresso | 1:2 | 18 g in, 36 g out in 25–30 s |
| AeroPress | 1:12–1:15 | 15 g, 200 g water |
| V60 / pour-over | 1:15–1:17 | 15 g, 250 g water |
| French press | 1:14–1:16 | 30 g, 450 g water |
| Cold brew concentrate | 1:5–1:8 | 100 g, 700 g cold water, 16 h |

## Water matters more than you think

A cup of coffee is 98.6 % water. The SCA recommends about 150 mg/l total hardness and a pH near 7. Very soft water extracts slowly and tastes flat; very hard water mutes acidity and scales your kettle. If your tap water is hard, try a jug filter or a bottled water with 50–150 mg/l of minerals.

> **Rule of thumb for the next chapter**
>
> Start at **1:16**, **93 °C**, a grind like coarse sand, and a total brew time of **3:00–3:30**. Taste. Too sour: grind finer. Too bitter: grind coarser. Too weak or too strong: change the ratio, not the grind.
