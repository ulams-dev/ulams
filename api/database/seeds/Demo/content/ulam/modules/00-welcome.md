::: richtext title="Welcome to the Scottish Book" preview=1 duration="8 min" intro="How the course works and what comes next."
**Open the Scottish Book.** In the 1930s, mathematicians in the Polish city of Lwów (today Lviv, in Ukraine) wrote down problems for one another in a notebook kept at a café. This course follows one of them, Stanisław Ulam, from Lwów to Los Alamos, and lets you try some of his ideas yourself in small live interactives before you read the explanation.

## How a lesson works

1. **Play with the interactive.** A map, a set of notebook cards, a spiral of numbers, a random experiment or a grid that grows. Try the idea first.
2. **Read the story.** A short text says what you found and where it came from. Every historical statement is numbered, and the list of sources is under the text.
3. **Practise and check.** A card set, a timeline, a table or a practice activity, then a short quiz. The quiz tells you straight away what you got right.

There are eight modules, a final test with a certificate and a last lesson that lists every source.

## How the facts are treated

- **Only checked statements.** Every sentence about a person, a date or a number was checked against a published source, and the sources are named in each lesson. Quotations are short and exact. Nothing in the course is a quotation unless it is in quotation marks.
- **Disagreements are shown, not hidden.** Where careful sources disagree, the course says so. You will meet two cases in module 2: nobody can say for certain who bought the notebook, or how it came through the war. Quizzes never ask about a point that is disputed.
- **Not a biography, not a verdict.** The course is a guided tour of a few ideas and the people around them. The last lesson says where to read more.

Every step of an interactive has a text version, so you can follow along without the graphics, with reduced motion, or with a screen reader.

Write the whole numbers on a square spiral, then press Next step to mark the primes: they crowd into diagonal lines. This is the Ulam spiral, and module 6 tells how it came about and lets you build one.

**Try this.** Look for the long diagonals. Do the primes seem to like some lines more than others?
