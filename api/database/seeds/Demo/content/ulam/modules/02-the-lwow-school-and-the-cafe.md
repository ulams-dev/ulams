::: richtext title="2.1 The Lwów School" duration="8 min" intro="Who the mathematicians were and what they worked on."
MacTutor describes the Lwów School as "a group of about a dozen mathematicians working in functional analysis, real functions and probability theory" {{src:U-07}}. People who met at the Scottish Café include Banach, Steinhaus, Ulam, Mazur, Kac, Schauder and Kaczmarz, and Władysław Orlicz himself {{src:U-35}}.

The school has a founding story. In 1916 Steinhaus met Banach in the Planty gardens in Kraków, after he "overheard the words 'Lebesgue measure'" {{src:U-10}}. Banach's 1920 dissertation "defined axiomatically what today is called a Banach space", and in 1932 his *Théorie des opérations linéaires* appeared, a French version of his 1931 Polish book {{src:U-09}}. In 1929 Banach and Steinhaus founded the journal *Studia Mathematica* {{src:U-09,U-10}}.

Ulam's teacher Kuratowski taught at the Lwów Polytechnic from 1927 and moved to the University of Warsaw in 1934 {{src:U-34}}. He had left Lwów before the mathematicians began writing down the problems in the Scottish Book, so he belongs to the teachers of this story, not to the café.

:::

::: richtext title="2.2 The café and the book" duration="12 min" intro="A notebook of problems, a head waiter, prizes in food and drink, and a war."
The Scottish Café stood in a building at 27 Shevchenko Avenue in today's Lviv, near the old university {{src:U-54}}. The mathematicians met there to talk over their problems, and at some point they began to write them down. In Ulam's words, the book "was started in Lwów, in Poland in 1935". Its first entry, Problem 1, is by Banach and dated 17 July 1935 {{src:U-12,U-33}}.

![The building at 27 Shevchenko Avenue in Lviv, where the Scottish Café was]({{asset:scottish-cafe-building.webp}})

*The building at 27 Shevchenko Avenue, Lviv. Photo: Rbrechko, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**The notebook.** Ulam remembered that Banach bought a large notebook and left it with the head waiter of the café, who brought it out on request {{src:U-12}}. Another account says that Banach's wife, Łucja, supplied it {{src:U-52}}. The sources differ, and the course does not choose.

**The prizes.** MacTutor's biography of Mazur says: "Prizes offered included wine, spirits, or a meal in Cambridge" {{src:U-07}}. For Problem 43, Mazur offered a bottle of wine. For Problem 152, Steinhaus offered 100 grammes of caviar for computing a frequency, a small beer for proving that it exists and a demitasse for a counterexample. Problem 151 carried a fondue in Geneva, and Samuel Eilenberg offered a bottle of wine for part (a) of Ulam's Problem 77 {{src:U-33}}. For Problem 184, in 1940, the prize was a kilogram of fatback; older English editions render it as bacon {{src:U-33}}.

**The war.** After the Soviet occupation began in 1939, visiting Soviet mathematicians, among them Bogolyubov, Alexandroff, Sobolev and Lusternik, added problems of their own {{src:U-12,U-33}}. The last entry, Problem 193, is by Steinhaus, dated 31 May 1941, and concerns the number of matches in a box {{src:U-12,U-35,U-10}}. In August 1939 Mazur told Ulam that if the city were bombed he would put the manuscripts and the book into a case and bury it {{src:U-12}}. That was a plan, not a record of what happened. Ulam wrote that the fate of the book during the remaining years of the war was not known to him: according to Steinhaus, Banach's son brought it to Wrocław {{src:U-12}}. MacTutor says instead that it seems to have been preserved through the war by Steinhaus, who sent it to Ulam {{src:U-10}}. Nobody wrote down exactly how it came through, and the course tells it as two accounts.

**After the war.** At Los Alamos, Ulam translated the book into English (1957) and circulated copies to mathematicians who asked for them {{src:U-12,U-33}}. R. Daniel Mauldin edited it with commentary for Birkhäuser in 1981, and again in 2015 {{src:U-15,U-33}}. Steinhaus started a *New Scottish Book* in Wrocław in 1946 {{src:U-10}}. Since 2014 a restaurant in the café building has used the old name and keeps a copy of the book, and a new "Lviv Scottish Book" of problems has grown from it {{src:U-33}}.

::: richtext title="2.3 Famous problems" duration="8 min" intro="Problem 153 and its goose."
Problem 153 was posed by Mazur on 6 November 1936. In plain words it is a question about whether certain Banach spaces have a basis, which turned out to be closely tied to the "approximation problem" for Banach spaces. Its prize was a live goose {{src:U-07}}. The answer was no: Per Enflo found a counterexample in 1972, published in 1973, and Mazur gave him the goose in Warsaw {{src:U-38,U-33}}.

The Scottish Book shows how long a good question can take. Problem 153 waited about thirty-six years. Problem 19, Ulam's question whether a body that floats in every position must be a sphere, was answered only in 2022 {{src:U-16}}. Problem 1 is still being worked on {{src:U-33}}. Problem 38 is seen today as a forerunner of random graph theory {{src:U-33}}.
