::: interactive title="Two masses" start=what-is-gravity end=what-is-gravity duration="5 min"
Gravity pulls any two masses together with a force F = G·m₁·m₂ / r² {{src:G-15}}. Between the Sun and the Earth that is about 3.5 × 10²² newtons, and it is exactly equal and opposite on both bodies. The Sun is about 333,000 times heavier than the Earth {{src:G-03}}, so the same force barely moves it.

**Try this.** Compare the two arrows. They have the same length. Why does the Earth still move so much more than the Sun?
:::

::: richtext title="One rule for every scale" duration="10 min"
**The law.** Newton's law of gravitation says that any two masses attract each other with a force F = G·m₁·m₂ / r². G is the gravitational constant, 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻² {{src:G-15}}. The force grows with each mass and falls with the square of the distance between them. Double the distance and the force becomes a quarter; triple the distance and it becomes a ninth.

**Equal and opposite.** The Sun pulls the Earth with a force of about 3.5 × 10²² newtons, and the Earth pulls the Sun with exactly the same force, as Newton's third law requires. The reason the Earth moves so much more is that its mass is small: a = F / m, and the Sun's mass is about 333,000 times the Earth's {{src:G-03}}.

**Mass and weight.** Mass is how much matter a body has; weight is the force with which gravity pulls on it. Your mass is the same everywhere; your weight on the Moon, whose surface gravity is about a sixth of the Earth's, would be about a sixth {{src:G-02}}.

**Building a solar system.** Gravity is not only a force between finished bodies. The early universe was almost smooth, to about one part in 100,000 {{src:G-56}}. Wherever the gas was slightly denser it pulled in more gas. About 4.6 billion years ago a cloud of gas and dust collapsed under its own gravity into a spinning disk with the Sun at its centre; dust grains stuck together and their growing gravity swept up more material, building planets {{src:G-33}}.
:::
