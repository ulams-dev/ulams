::: interactive title="Curved spacetime and Mercury" start=spacetime end=mercury-precession duration="7 min"
In general relativity (1915) mass and energy curve space and time, and a body in free fall follows the straightest path through the curved space. Mercury's orbit is the proof: its perihelion creeps around by 43 arcseconds per century more than Newton predicts, and relativity predicts exactly that {{src:G-31}}.

**Try this.** The effect is exaggerated enormously. Watch the ellipse turn and trace a rosette: what does the blue line mark?
:::

::: interactive title="Bent light and slow clocks" start=lensing end=time-dilation duration="7 min"
Light follows the curve too. In the 1919 eclipse, Eddington measured stars shifted near the Sun's edge and Einstein became famous {{src:G-32}}. Time slows deeper in a gravity well: GPS satellites must correct their clocks by about 38 microseconds a day {{src:G-37}}.

**Try this.** Compare the clock beside the mass with the distant one. Which falls behind?
:::

Inside an event horizon, escape would need a speed above that of light. On 14 September 2015 LIGO detected ripples from two black holes that merged about 1.3 billion light-years away {{src:G-27}}.

:::

::: richtext title="Newton, then Einstein" duration="12 min"
**Newton still works.** Newton's law gives orbits to a high precision. Einstein's general relativity (1915) goes deeper: mass and energy curve space and time, and a free-falling body follows the straightest path through that curve. When gravity is weak and speeds are small compared with light, the two agree.

**Mercury.** The closest point of Mercury's orbit moves a little each lap. After the pull of the other planets is taken into account Newton's theory leaves 43 arcseconds per century unexplained, and general relativity gives 42.98 {{src:G-31}}.

**Light and the 1919 eclipse.** If space is curved, light follows it, and the Sun should deflect starlight at its edge. In 1919 Eddington's expedition measured the shift during a total eclipse {{src:G-32}}. The quality of those 1919 measurements has been discussed, and later experiments have confirmed the effect many times.

**Clocks.** A clock in a deeper gravity well ticks slower. GPS satellites orbit higher, where gravity is weaker, so their clocks run faster by 45 microseconds a day, and their speed slows them by 7, a net of about 38 microseconds a day. Without a correction the positions would drift by about 10 km a day {{src:G-37}}.

**Black holes and waves.** Pack enough mass into a small enough space and the well has no bottom: not even light escapes. Sagittarius A* has about four million solar masses {{src:G-29}}. When two black holes spiral together they send ripples of spacetime outward at the speed of light. LIGO detected such a signal, GW150914, on 14 September 2015; it was announced on 11 February 2016, and the source lay about 1.3 billion light-years away {{src:G-27}} {{src:G-28}}.
:::
